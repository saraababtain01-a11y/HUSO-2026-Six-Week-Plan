Six weeks to the open round

Two taught sessions a week

Roughly 60–75 minutes each. The first session builds the concept, the second is problems on the board with students at the front.

Four study blocks a week

Two reading blocks of about 40 minutes and two problem blocks of about 45 minutes, done at home between sessions.

One timed set every Thursday

20–25 minutes, closed book except the official reference sheet. Mark it together at the start of the next session.

An error log per student

One page per week: the question, the wrong step, the correct idea in a single line. This is the only revision material needed in week 6.

Biology

The syllabus mirrors INBO content and the ICSE grade 9–10 curriculum, with Campbell as the reference text. Breadth matters more than depth here: almost every listed organ system can appear, so the six weeks sweep the whole list once and then drill recall.

Week1

Teach

Biomolecules and the cell

  • Carbon as the basis of life; nucleic acids, lipids, proteins, carbohydrates — structure tied to function.
  • Cell theory, then a guided tour of every organelle on the list, ending with why compartments exist at all.
  • Prokaryote vs eukaryote, plant vs animal cell, drawn as two comparison tables students build themselves.
  • Microscopy: magnification, resolution, what each part of the light microscope does.

Study

  • Campbell chapters on the chemistry of life and the cell; read the chapter review summaries first, then the chapter.
  • Make one organelle sheet: name, structure, function, present in plant / animal / both.
  • Label a blank plant cell and a blank animal cell from memory, twice.
Timed set: 15 multiple-choice questions on cell structure from past INBO or USABO open papers.

Quick check

  1. Which biomolecule is the primary structural component of cell membranes?
    1. Carbohydrates
    2. Proteins
    3. Lipids
    4. Nucleic acids
  2. Which organelle produces most of a cell's ATP through aerobic respiration?
    1. Golgi apparatus
    2. Mitochondria
    3. Lysosome
    4. Ribosome
  3. A cell with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles is best classified as:
    1. Eukaryotic
    2. Prokaryotic
    3. A plant cell
    4. An animal cell
  4. Which structure is found in plant cells but not animal cells?
    1. Mitochondria
    2. Cell membrane
    3. Cell wall
    4. Ribosome

Answers: 1–c, 2–b, 3–b, 4–c

Week2

Teach

Central dogma and cell division

  • DNA replication step by step, with the enzymes named and the leading/lagging distinction drawn.
  • Transcription, RNA processing, translation — one continuous worked example from gene to protein.
  • Mitosis and meiosis side by side; chromosome number at every stage.
  • Common exam traps: reading frames, complementary vs mRNA sequence, crossing over placement.

Study

  • Practise writing the mRNA and amino acid sequence from a DNA strand until it takes under a minute.
  • Draw the eight stages of meiosis unaided and annotate where variation is generated.
  • Error log: every question missed because of a stage name rather than a concept.
Timed set: 15 questions on molecular biology and division.

Quick check

  1. Copying the information in DNA into a strand of mRNA is called:
    1. Translation
    2. Transcription
    3. Replication
    4. Transduction
  2. During which stage of mitosis do chromosomes line up at the cell's equator?
    1. Prophase
    2. Metaphase
    3. Anaphase
    4. Telophase
  3. Meiosis of a single parent cell produces how many daughter cells?
    1. 2
    2. 3
    3. 4
    4. 8
  4. Which enzyme unwinds the DNA double helix during replication?
    1. DNA polymerase
    2. Helicase
    3. Ligase
    4. Primase

Answers: 1–b, 2–b, 3–c, 4–b

Week3

Teach

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration

  • Photosynthesis: overall equation, light-dependent and light-independent stages, where each occurs.
  • The four limiting factors and how each one bends the rate graph.
  • Respiration: aerobic and anaerobic word equations, in plants as well as animals, and the energy yield of each.
  • Classic demonstration set-ups — destarched leaf, hydrogencarbonate indicator, germinating seeds — as they are examined.

Study

  • Build a single comparison table: photosynthesis vs respiration, across site, reactants, products, energy, timing.
  • Sketch and explain three rate graphs from the limiting-factor work.
  • Write out both experiment protocols including the control.
Timed set: 12 questions, at least four of them experiment-based.

Quick check

  1. The light-independent reactions of photosynthesis (the Calvin cycle) take place in the:
    1. Thylakoid membrane
    2. Stroma
    3. Cytoplasm
    4. Mitochondrial matrix
  2. Which gas is released as a product of photosynthesis?
    1. Carbon dioxide
    2. Nitrogen
    3. Oxygen
    4. Methane
  3. Anaerobic respiration in yeast produces ethanol and:
    1. Oxygen
    2. Carbon dioxide
    3. Water
    4. Lactic acid
  4. Which pigment absorbs light for photosynthesis?
    1. Melanin
    2. Chlorophyll
    3. Keratin
    4. Haemoglobin

Answers: 1–b, 2–c, 3–b, 4–b

Week4

Teach

Tissues, digestion, gas exchange, excretion

  • Epithelial, connective and the three muscle types — location and one function each, as the syllabus specifies.
  • Digestive organs and glands, food classes, balanced diet, deficiency diseases.
  • Breathing mechanism, tissue respiration, heat production.
  • Excretory organs and their products; the kidney and urinary tract with a labelled diagram. Nephron structure is explicitly out of scope — say so.

Study

  • One page per system: organ, function, secretion where relevant.
  • Label the digestive tract and the urinary system from blank diagrams.
  • Learn the deficiency-disease list as a table, not as prose.
Timed set: 15 questions mixing tissues, digestion and excretion.

    1. Pancreas

    3. Stomach
    4. Small intestine
  3. The main nitrogenous waste removed by the kidneys is:
    1. Glucose

Week5

Teach

Nerves, hormones, blood, immunity

  • Brain, spinal cord and nerves; cerebrum, cerebellum and medulla by function; motor neuron structure.
  • Reflex arc with every term defined: stimulus, receptor, impulse, response, effector.
  • Exocrine vs endocrine; the four listed glands in the tabular form the syllabus asks for.
  • Heart internal structure, double circulation, named vessels, blood groups, and the lymphatic system as a parallel circuit.
  • Innate vs adaptive immunity, B and T cells, antibodies, allergy and allergens.

Study

  • Draw the heart with valves and septum, then trace a red blood cell through both circuits.
  • Complete the four-gland table: name, location, secretion, function.
  • Memorise the universal donor and acceptor logic rather than the answer.
Timed set: 18 questions across all four systems.

Quick check

  1. Which part of the brain is mainly responsible for balance and coordination?
    1. Cerebrum
    2. Cerebellum
    3. Medulla oblongata
    4. Spinal cord
  2. A person with which blood type is the universal donor?
    1. A
    2. B
    3. AB
    4. O
  3. Which cells are mainly responsible for producing antibodies?
    1. T cells
    2. B cells
    3. Red blood cells
    4. Platelets
  4. In a reflex arc, which structure carries the impulse from the spinal cord to the muscle?
    1. Receptor
    2. Sensory neuron
    3. Motor neuron
    4. Effector

Week6

Teach

Ecology, classification, and the mock

  • Food chains, food webs, pyramid of numbers; symbiosis, parasitism, predation.

Study

  • Revise only from the error log and the comparison tables built in weeks 1–5.
  • One past INBO open paper on Monday, one USABO open paper on Thursday, both timed.

Chemistry

A periodic table and all needed constants are supplied in the exam, so nothing here is a memory test of numbers. Common formulas and reactions do have to be known. Organic chemistry is capped at roughly 15–20% of the paper, which is exactly how much time it gets in this plan.

InorganicAnalyticalThermochemistryOrganic ~15–20%Lab skills
Week1

Teach

Atoms, isotopes, electron configuration

  • Atomic number, mass number, relative atomic mass, isotope calculations.
  • Alpha, beta and gamma decay; writing and balancing nuclear equations.
  • Quantum numbers n, l, m; s, p and d orbitals; Hund's rule and the Pauli principle.
  • Configurations of atoms and ions, then paramagnetism vs diamagnetism as a direct consequence.

Study

  • Write configurations for twenty elements and ten ions, including the transition-metal exceptions.
  • Balance fifteen nuclear equations.
  • Work three weighted-average atomic mass problems in both directions.
Timed set: 12 questions from a USNCO local paper, atomic structure only.

Quick check

  1. Isotopes of the same element differ in their number of:
    1. Protons
    2. Electrons
    3. Neutrons
    4. Valence electrons
  2. Which subatomic particle carries no electric charge?
    1. Proton
    2. Electron
    3. Neutron
    4. Ion
  3. The electron configuration of neon (Z = 10) is:
    1. 1s² 2s² 2p⁶
    2. 1s² 2s² 2p⁴
    3. 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²
    4. 1s² 2p⁸
  4. Which type of radioactive decay involves the emission of a helium nucleus?
    1. Alpha
    2. Beta
    3. Gamma
    4. Positron

Answers: 1–c, 2–c, 3–a, 4–a

Week2

Teach

Periodic trends and bonding

  • Main group vs transition elements; the six trends on the syllabus, each explained by shielding and nuclear charge rather than memorised.
  • Bond types, hybridisation, VSEPR: shape, bond angle, polarity.
  • Molecules worked to completion: Lewis structure, steric number, shape, polarity.

Study

  • Complete a VSEPR grid of twenty molecules and ions across all the standard geometries.
  • For each trend, write the one-sentence reason it exists and the one common exception.
  • Predict polarity for fifteen molecules and check against the shape.
Timed set: 15 questions on trends and molecular geometry.

Quick check

  1. Moving left to right across a period, atomic radius generally:
    1. Increases
    2. Decreases
    3. Stays the same
    4. Increases then decreases
  2. Which type of bond involves atoms sharing pairs of electrons?
    1. Ionic
    2. Covalent
    3. Metallic
    4. Hydrogen
  3. A central atom with a tetrahedral electron geometry and no lone pairs has bond angles of approximately:
    1. 90°
    2. 109.5°
    3. 120°
    4. 180°
  4. Ionisation energy generally increases moving:
    1. Down a group
    2. Up a group, right to left
    3. Down a group, right to left
    4. Up a group and left to right across a period

Answers: 1–b, 2–b, 3–b, 4–d

Week3

Teach

Intermolecular forces, solids, reaction types

  • The forces ranked, and the boiling-point questions they generate.
  • Metallic bonding and the properties it explains; acid–base behaviour of oxides across a period.
  • Reaction types and balancing, including redox by oxidation number.
  • Empirical and molecular formulas from composition data.

Study

  • Balance thirty equations of mixed type, ten of them redox.
  • Rank five sets of compounds by boiling point and justify each ranking in one line.
  • Five empirical-formula problems from combustion data.
Timed set: 15 questions on bonding, forces and reaction types.

Quick check

  1. Of the following, which intermolecular force is generally the strongest?
    1. London dispersion
    2. Dipole–dipole
    3. Hydrogen bonding
    4. They are always equal in strength
  2. A reaction in which one compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances is called:
    1. Synthesis
    2. Decomposition
    3. Single replacement
    4. Double replacement
  3. In metallic bonding, the valence electrons are best described as:
    1. Localised on one atom
    2. Delocalised across the metal (a 'sea of electrons')
    3. Completely transferred
    4. Shared in fixed pairs only
  4. A compound is 40% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O by mass. Its empirical formula is:
    1. CH₂O
    2. C₂H₄O₂
    3. C₆H₁₂O₆
    4. CHO

Answers: 1–c, 2–b, 3–b, 4–a

Week4

Teach

Stoichiometry

  • The mole through volume, mass and number of atoms — the three routes on one diagram.
  • Limiting reagent, percentage yield, percentage purity.
  • Gas stoichiometry with the ideal gas law using the supplied value of R.
  • Concentration calculations and dilution.

Study

  • Twenty-five mixed stoichiometry problems, worked with units carried through every line.
  • Rewrite three problems you got wrong as a clean model solution for the error log.
  • Practise unit conversion speed: g to mol to particles to litres at STP.
Timed set: 12 calculation questions, 20 minutes, calculator discipline enforced.

Quick check

  1. One mole of any substance contains how many particles (Avogadro's number)?
    1. 6.022 × 10²²
    2. 6.022 × 10²³
    3. 3.011 × 10²³
    4. 1.0 × 10²³
  2. For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, how many moles of water form from 4 moles of H₂ with excess O₂?
    1. 2
    2. 4
    3. 6
    4. 8
  3. A reaction has a theoretical yield of 50 g and an actual yield of 40 g. The percentage yield is:
    1. 40%
    2. 60%
    3. 80%
    4. 90%
  4. Using PV = nRT, if pressure and temperature are held constant, doubling the volume will:
    1. Double the number of moles
    2. Halve the number of moles
    3. Not change the number of moles present
    4. Quadruple the number of moles

Answers: 1–b, 2–b, 3–c, 4–a

Week5

Teach

Solutions, acids and bases

  • Molarity, molality and the rest — when each one is the right measure.
  • Self-ionisation of water, Kw, pH and pOH.
  • Strong and weak acids and bases; hydrolysis of salts and why a salt solution is not neutral.
  • Neutralisation and titration arithmetic, plus the lab skills the paper asks about: apparatus choice, procedure, error analysis.

Study

  • Twenty pH problems: strong acid, strong base, weak acid, salt solution.
  • Write the hydrolysis equation for eight salts and predict acidic, basic or neutral.
  • For three given experiments, name the apparatus, the main error source and how to reduce it.
Timed set: 15 questions on solutions and acid–base chemistry.

Physics

Mechanics, electromagnetism and thermodynamics are named as the main subjects, with acoustics added. Constants and a formula table are provided in the exam, and anything unusual is given in the question itself — so sessions should spend their time on setting problems up, not on recall.

Week1

Teach

Kinematics and vectors

  • Scalars and vectors; components, addition, relative velocity.
  • The three kinematic equations from the sheet, and how to choose between them in one reading of the problem.
  • Graphs: position, velocity and acceleration read off each other.
  • Projectile motion as two independent one-dimensional problems.

Study

  • Morin, the introductory chapters — read the worked solution only after attempting the problem.
  • Twenty kinematics problems, ten of them projectile.
  • Translate five motion graphs into words and five descriptions into graphs.

    1. 2.5 m/s

    4. 20 m/s
  4. On a position–time graph, the slope at any point represents the object's:
    1. Acceleration

Answers: 1–c, 2–c, 3–c, 4–b

Week2

Teach

Forces, work and energy

  • Free-body diagrams as the first line of every solution, without exception.

Study

Week3

Teach

Momentum, circular motion, gravity

  • Impulse and momentum; elastic and inelastic collisions in one and two dimensions.
  • Centre of mass and why it simplifies collision problems.
  • Uniform circular motion, centripetal acceleration, banked curves, vertical loops.
  • Newtonian gravity, orbits, and gravitational potential energy.

Study

  • Fifteen collision problems; for each, state which quantities are conserved before solving.
  • Ten circular motion problems, including the minimum-speed-at-the-top type.
  • Five orbital problems using the supplied value of G.
Timed set: 12 questions on conservation laws.

Week4

Teach

Rotation and oscillation

  • Torque, angular acceleration, and the moments of inertia given on the sheet.
  • The parallel axis theorem — the sample question on a stick about its end is exactly this.
  • Angular momentum and its conservation; rolling without slipping.
  • Simple harmonic motion: mass–spring and pendulum periods, energy in oscillation.

Study

  • Derive the stick-about-end result from the centre result, then do three more parallel-axis cases.
  • Twelve rotation problems and eight SHM problems.
  • Learn to read the moment-of-inertia table fast — the exam supplies it, but only under time pressure.
Timed set: 12 questions on rotation and SHM.

Week5

Teach

Thermodynamics

  • The three laws, stated plainly and then applied.
  • Heat transfer, heat capacity, specific heat, latent heat, phases and phase transitions.
  • The ideal gas law with the supplied R; isothermal, isobaric and adiabatic processes.
  • Work done by a gas — the sample question on 7 moles expanding at constant temperature is worked in full.

Study

  • Fifteen calorimetry and phase-change problems.
  • Ten gas-law problems; always convert to kelvin as the first written line.
  • Sketch PV diagrams for each process type and shade the work done.

Week6

Teach

Circuits, waves, optics, and the mock

  • Coulomb's law, electric field and potential difference from the formula sheet.
  • Sound: loudness, pitch, frequency, longitudinal waves, reflection; v = λf.

  • Full mock, then review of the first ten questions for speed and the last ten for method.

  • Ten wave and sound questions, five optics questions.
  • Two full Physics Bowl papers under time.
